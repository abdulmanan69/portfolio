# Clean, Minimal Web Design — Less but Better

"Minimal" gets misread as "empty." But a good minimal design isn't a page with
nothing on it — it's a page with *nothing extra* on it. Every element earns its
place, and the ones that stay get room to breathe.

## Start with hierarchy, not decoration

Before colours and shadows, I ask: what should the eye hit first, second, third?
Type size, weight and spacing do most of that work. If the hierarchy is clear, the
page already looks designed — before I've added a single flourish.

## One accent, used with restraint

I usually pick **one** accent colour and let everything else be ink and paper. When
only one thing is bright, that brightness means something. On this site it's a warm
coral — it marks links, highlights, and the little details worth noticing.

> Constraint is a feature. A limited palette makes every choice louder.

## Whitespace is not wasted space

Generous spacing is the cheapest way to make something feel premium. It slows the
reader down, groups related things, and separates unrelated ones — no borders
required.

## Motion should whisper

Interaction and minimalism aren't enemies. A cursor that trails softly, a card that
lifts a few pixels on hover, text that fades up as you scroll — these say "this is
alive" without shouting. The moment motion distracts from the content, it's too much.

## Less, but better

Dieter Rams said it best: *good design is as little design as possible.* Cut until
it hurts, then add back only what the content truly needs. What's left is calm,
fast, and honest — which is exactly how I want my work to feel.
